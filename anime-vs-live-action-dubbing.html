Why Drawn Mouths Change the Rules

Anime and live action both need lip-sync. But when the mouth on screen was drawn instead of filmed, the job changes. Some rules disappear. Others get stricter than ever.

Essay by Djamel Bennecib

I've adapted Japanese live action and I've adapted Japanese anime. Same source language, same kind of dialogue list, same software open on my screen. And yet it doesn't feel like the same job at all.

People tend to assume animation is the easy version of dubbing. No real faces, no real lips, so no real constraints. That's half true. Some of the hardest rules of live action simply don't apply to a drawn mouth. But animation brings its own demands, and they're less forgiving than most people think.

The Mouth Was Never Japanese

Here's the first thing to understand. In most anime, the voices are recorded after the timing of the animation has already been set, sometimes to finished animation, sometimes to rough or incomplete visuals. The Japanese actors stand in the booth and fit their performance to mouths and cuts that already have a rhythm. Those mouths weren't built around Japanese sounds. They were built around timing: when a character starts talking, how long they talk, when they stop.

That changes everything for the adapter. In live action, the original actor's lips are shaped by the original language. Every consonant left a mark on the picture, and my English has to live with those marks. In anime, the Japanese dialogue was already an adaptation of sorts, fitted to a drawing. When I write the English version, I'm standing in the exact same place the Japanese actors stood.

I'm not matching a language. I'm matching a drawing.

What Disappears

In live action, a close-up is a contract. If the actor's lips press together on screen, the English line needs an M, a B or a P right there. If the lower lip touches the teeth, I need an F or a V. Labial mapping is a big part of the work, and in a tight close-up it can decide every word choice in a line.

A drawn mouth usually doesn't work that way. Most anime mouths cycle through a handful of shapes: closed, half open, open. There are no lips pressing together on a specific consonant, because nobody animated a specific consonant. So a whole layer of constraint, the one that often forces the most painful compromises in live action, mostly falls away.

That's the part everyone notices. It's also where the misunderstanding starts.

What Gets Stricter

When the shape of the mouth stops mattering, the movement of the mouth becomes everything. Where it opens. How many times it flaps. Where it closes. In anime, those moments are drawn frame by frame, and they're completely unambiguous. The mouth is either moving or it isn't.

A real actor gives you a little room. A breath before a line, a lip that trembles, a head that turns slightly away. Real faces are messy, and that mess can hide a syllable or two. A drawn face can't hide anything. If the character's mouth keeps moving after my English line has ended, the audience sees it instantly. If the voice starts before the mouth opens, it looks broken.

Anime forgives the wrong shape. It never forgives the wrong length.

Here's something I run into on almost every anime episode. In the original, the Japanese line often ends while the character's mouth keeps moving. The voice stops, the flaps don't. In Japanese, that mismatch is simply part of the performance.

In English, I can't leave that space empty. If the mouth moves, I need something there, whether it's words, a reaction or a breath, all the way to the last flap. So I regularly end up with an English line that already says exactly what the character means, and it's still not finished. It's two or three flaps short. The meaning is done. The picture isn't.

As long as the mouth moves, the line isn't over.

That's why a good English anime adaptation sometimes needs more verbal material than the original. Not because I like extra words, but because the drawing asks for them.

So the flap count and the in and out points become central to the job. I'm writing to a rhythm that's been locked on paper, not to a performance that breathes.

The Freedom Trap

Losing the labial constraint feels like freedom. And it is, for word choice. But freedom on the mouth is not freedom on meaning, and that's where anime adaptation can go wrong.

Anime audiences are some of the most attentive viewers there are. Many of them have seen the subtitled version first. They know the characters' names, the way those characters address each other, their catchphrases, the names of their techniques. When a series runs past a hundred episodes, all of that becomes established canon. The adapter isn't just writing a scene. They're keeping a world consistent that thousands of people know by heart.

So the extra room I get on sounds doesn't go into rewriting. It goes into precision: finding the English line that matches the timing, respects what's already been set up, and still sounds like something a person would actually say.

The Performance Is Drawn Bigger

Anime acting is not naturalistic, and it isn't supposed to be. Characters gasp, shout, laugh too loud, react with their whole bodies. A lot of that lives outside the dialogue: the little sounds, the surprised reactions, the efforts during a fight. In the original, many of those are short Japanese interjections that don't mean anything word for word but carry the whole emotion of the moment.

Those have to be adapted too. An English reaction that feels natural, fills the flaps and matches the size of the drawing. A scream needs open vowels the actor can push. A whisper needs sounds that stay soft. If I write a small, realistic line for a character whose face is drawn at full volume, the voice and the picture pull apart, and the scene loses its energy.

Live Action: The Face Is the Text

Go back to live action and everything reverses. The shapes matter again, but so does something no drawing gives you: a real person was there. Their breathing, their hesitations, the micro-expressions that come half a second before they speak. The performance already exists in full, and my job is to write English that slips inside it without anyone noticing.

In live action, I adapt to an actor. In anime, I adapt to an animator, and the voice actor in the booth will finish what the drawing only suggests. That's a different kind of responsibility. With a drawn character, the English line isn't just matching a performance. It's part of building one.

Two Disciplines, One Goal

After years of doing both, I don't think of one as harder than the other. They just ask for different things. Live action asks you to disappear into someone else's performance. Anime asks you to give the voice actor something that makes a drawing feel alive.

But the goal never changes. The audience shouldn't think about the adaptation at all. They should just believe the character is speaking.

Whether the mouth was filmed or drawn, the line has to be playable.
